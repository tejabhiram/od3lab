Research

Delivery problems, clinical diseases

The eye is easy to see and hard to dose. Tears wash drugs away within minutes, the cornea blocks most molecules, and the back of the eye is reached only by injection. We design materials that overcome these barriers, and we use the host response to infection to tell clinicians which pathogen they are treating.

01
NEI R01EY034508

Sustained topical delivery with DECON

DECON (drug-encapsulated carbon) is a porous activated-carbon platform first described in Science Advances in 2019. Its high surface area and mucoadhesive surface let it load antivirals and corticosteroids, stay on the ocular surface and release drug slowly.

Our NEI-funded work asks whether DECON can deliver dexamethasone through the inflammatory phase that follows viral keratitis, replacing frequent steroid drops with a single sustained dose.

  • Drug loading, retention and release in vitro, ex vivo and in vivo
  • Suppression of infectious and non-infectious ocular inflammation using clinically relevant metrics
  • Nanoparticle DECON formulations for intracellular antiviral delivery
Funded by the National Eye Institute, R01EY034508 (PI Yadavalli), 2023 to 2028
02
Microneedles

Dissolving microneedles for the cornea

Eye drops reach the corneal stroma poorly. Microneedles shorter than the corneal thickness can place drug directly into the stroma, where it forms a depot, without a hypodermic injection.

Our lead platform, O-GPT (ophthalmic general purpose therapeutic), is a dissolvable microneedle film designed to improve compliance and reduce the burden of frequent dosing for patients with chronic and infectious corneal disease.

  • Fabrication of dissolving and bilayer microneedle films
  • Corneal penetration, dissolution and stromal drug distribution
  • Delivery of small molecules and biologics

UIC CCTS pilot UIC OVCR

Polymeric depots, films and fibers

Biodegradable polymers such as PLGA can hold drug for weeks. We tune their composition and geometry for specific ocular sites and diseases.

  • PLGA microspheres loaded with acyclovir and ganciclovir for sustained treatment of herpetic uveitis, evaluated for safety, pharmacokinetics and efficacy in mouse models
  • Dual-drug PLGA thin films that co-release an antibiotic and a steroid after ocular surgery, with quantitative release modeling
  • Electrospun PLGA fibers for post-surgical complications, with materials collaborators
  • Polymer-free steroid microparticles for sustained dexamethasone release
Supported by a UIC CCTS pilot award (2025 to 2026) and UIC OVCR institute funding (2024)
04
Machine learning

Host-directed pathogen classification

Infectious keratitis, uveitis and endophthalmitis are treated by pathogen class, yet culture, microscopy, PCR and metagenomic sequencing all depend on finding enough pathogen material in tiny ocular samples. The host response is a far more abundant signal. Viruses, fungi and bacteria each switch on distinct transcriptional programs in the cells they infect.

We train machine learning classifiers on these host transcriptomic signatures to identify the infecting pathogen without detecting it directly. In a proof-of-concept study we assembled 330 human RNA-seq samples from 36 independent studies covering six ocular pathogens: HSV-1, human cytomegalovirus, Aspergillus fumigatus, Candida albicans, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Using reproducible "robust" genes and strict leave-one-study-out validation, classifiers identified the pathogen well above chance, and pathogens from different kingdoms separated most clearly.

  • Cross-study robust gene signatures for viral, fungal and bacterial infection
  • Leakage-aware validation that reflects real-world performance
  • Host signals that shape monocyte responses in HSV-1 stromal keratitis
  • Next step, validation in ocular tissue and clinical specimens
